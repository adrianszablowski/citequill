# A full site SEO audit with a score: what to check, what it costs, what the crawl already gives

Research date: 17 October 2026. Prompted by the owner asking for a site score from 0 to 100, "really thick": meta titles and descriptions, alt text with the exact image named, JSON-LD, sitemaps, noindex and nofollow, accessibility, "everything I did not list", at a cost close to free, since an audit runs once in a while. Four research threads were merged: the check catalogues of the known tools and how they score; the cost of crawling a whole site on Vercel; images, alt text and accessibility; indexability, structured data and international rules. Grades: A official Google, W3C, schema.org, sitemaps.org, RFC or the tool's own documentation or source code; B independent test, WebAIM, Deque, MDN or a well-known SEO publication citing primary data; C vendor page; D blog, forum or trade press.

## The short answer

Yes, it can be close to free. A fetch-and-parse crawl of a 5 000 page site costs about 3 to 4 cents of Vercel compute per run, a 1 000 page site under one cent, a 100 page site a tenth of a cent, and the $20 monthly Pro credit absorbs all of it (A, Vercel pricing). Fetching the customer's pages has no published bandwidth charge. The Google APIs that add performance and index data are free with daily quotas. The limit is time, not money: at a polite 2 requests a second a 5 000 page crawl takes about 42 minutes, longer than one function may run, so it is a Vercel Workflow of batched steps, as the connect crawl already is.

What is not free, and the decisions behind it:

- JavaScript-only sites. A page whose HTML is an empty application shell has to be rendered in a headless browser to be audited. Rendering costs 10 to 50 times the fetch, still only about $0.10 to $0.50 per 1 000 pages, but it is a second engine to run and package. Recommendation: render a sample only when the raw HTML fails a shell test, and report the gap itself as a finding.
- The audit cannot name the source file of an image. A crawler sees the rendered HTML, so it reports the page URL, the image `src`, the alt value as found, a CSS selector and a short HTML snippet. Naming the component or template that produced it needs code on the customer's side, which is the Next.js package in the backlog.
- Field Core Web Vitals usually do not exist for a small site. Chrome's CrUX data covers only origins above an undisclosed popularity threshold (A). The score must say "no field data" and leave those points out, never score 0.

The owner's other questions, answered one by one:

- Is there a convention for "this image needs no alt"? Yes: the empty alt, `alt=""`. The HTML standard requires exactly that for a decorative image (A). `role="presentation"`, `role="none"` and `aria-hidden="true"` also mark an image as decorative, and a CSS background image is not an image to an auditor at all. The error is a missing alt attribute, never an empty one. Every standards-based checker agrees; only Ahrefs has a history of flagging `alt=""`, which its users complained about from 2020 to 2026 (C, D).
- Does accessibility affect ranking? No, not directly. John Mueller, March 2022: accessibility is "not something that we would pick up and use as a direct ranking factor" (D, trade-press transcript). What overlaps is small and specific: alt text, link text, the page title, Core Web Vitals, the viewport tag. So the audit checks the overlap and reports the rest as hints outside the score.
- Can the audit piggyback on the connect crawl? Partly. The guarded fetch, the sitemap and robots readers, cheerio and the signal extraction are reusable as they stand. The 50 page budget, the ranking of pages, the 15 minute stop rule and the 2 MB cut are the connect crawl's rules and do not fit an audit that must see every URL. The audit is its own run with its own budget, sharing the lower layer.
- What does 100 mean? Every page Google can index is indexable, reachable by links, served over HTTPS, titled, with a viewport and valid structured data where there is any, and nothing in the spam policies applies. It does not mean every title is under 60 characters or every page has one H1, because Google says neither matters.
- The GEO half. A "full" audit for a product that sells AI visibility checks robots.txt for the AI search agents (OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, PerplexityBot), fetches the home page with their user agents to catch a CDN block, and checks `nosnippet`, which keeps a page out of AI Overviews. `llms.txt` is reported as present or absent and never scored: Google says it neither helps nor harms (A).

Recommendation on placement: after the MVP's publishing step, as its own section in the sidebar, run on demand and then monthly. The cost makes monthly runs for every customer negligible. The reason to wait is scope, not money: the catalogue below is about 150 checks, and the first version should ship the 40 or so that Google documents as requirements or recommendations.

### Worked estimates

Assumptions: 2 requests a second per host, 50 ms of CPU per page for fetch handling, parsing and 30 checks, 50 pages per Workflow step, 2 KB of findings per page.

| Pages | Wall time | Memory  | Active CPU | Workflow | Total per crawl |
| ----- | --------- | ------- | ---------- | -------- | --------------- |
| 100   | 50 s      | $0.0003 | $0.0002    | $0.0002  | about $0.0006   |
| 1 000 | 8 min     | $0.0029 | $0.0018    | $0.0022  | about $0.006    |
| 5 000 | 42 min    | $0.0147 | $0.0089    | $0.011   | about $0.035    |

Add 20% for cold starts and retries. One hundred customers with 1 000 pages each, audited monthly, cost under $1 of compute and sit inside the credit. Two caveats: whether Pro lets memory go below 2 GB was not confirmed, and Fluid compute's instance sharing would lower the memory line further.

### Design consequences

- Batch about 50 pages per step. One step per page at 5 000 pages would be 15 000 events, past the 2 000 line where Workflow replay slows (A).
- Pass only findings between steps, never HTML: step outputs are persisted and billed per GB.
- Store one row per page with a JSONB map of checks, plus a `findings` table for failures only. 5 000 pages by 30 checks is about 5 to 10 MB per audit that way, against 25 MB as one row per check (A, Postgres tuple layout).
- Keep a content hash per page so the next audit can tell what changed. Store gzipped HTML only for pages with findings, with a 90 day lifecycle, so the owner can see the markup behind a failure. Supabase Storage or Vercel Blob at about 2 to 4 cents per GB month either way (A).

### Rendering

Google crawls raw HTML first and queues pages for rendering "once Google's resources allow"; it says "not all bots can run JavaScript" and that server-side or pre-rendering "is still a great idea" (A). Framer pre-renders every site at publication (A); Webflow serves static HTML from a CDN (C). A client-side React app without SSR gives an empty root element and needs rendering.

| Route                                  | Cost per rendered page            | Note                                                                     | Grade |

Rule: render only when the raw HTML fails a shell test (an empty `#root`, `#__next` or `#app` with under about 200 characters of visible text, or a `<noscript>` saying JavaScript is required, or sitemap URLs the HTML crawl never linked to). Then render the home page, one page per URL pattern and at most 50 pages. The raw-versus-rendered gap is a finding of its own, because the raw view is what most crawlers and all AI fetchers see.

### Politeness and getting in

- Fetch robots.txt first, cache it up to 24 hours, honour `Crawl-delay` when present even though Google ignores it (A, RFC 9309 and Bing). 4xx means no restrictions; 5xx means stop.
- 1 to 2 requests a second per host, concurrency 2 to 4, back off and honour `Retry-After` on 429 or 503, hard stop after a run of 5xx (A, Google's reduce-crawl-rate page).
- Keep the named user agent with a URL, and publish a page that says what the bot does and how to block or verify it.
- Cloudflare Free with Bot Fight Mode cannot be bypassed by rule; the owner turns it off or upgrades (A). Pro and above take a WAF skip rule. Vercel's firewall takes a bypass rule (A). In both the match should be a shared-secret header Citequill sends per site, because function IPs change and a user agent is spoofable (A). When the customer will not change settings, the audit reports "blocked by bot protection" with the status codes seen.
- Obey robots.txt by default and offer an "ignore robots.txt for my own site" switch that records the owner's choice.

## 2. What the connect crawl already gives

From `features/crawl/`: the guarded fetch (public hosts only, 5 hops by hand, 15 s, 2 MB), sitemap discovery from robots.txt and `/sitemap.xml`, the sitemap and index parser, cheerio, the markdown conversion, and `readSignals()`: description, `og:site_name`, `og:title`, `og:description`, canonical, JSON-LD nodes of the types of interest, mailto and tel links, social links, footer text. `readPage()` also gives the title, `<html lang>`, hreflang alternates, the author, all links and the header, navigation and footer links.

## 3. The catalogue

About 150 distinct checks, merged from Screaming Frog's published issue list, Semrush's 100 issues, Sitebulb's hints, Lighthouse's SEO audits and Ahrefs' help centre (A for each tool's own list; Ahrefs publishes no complete catalogue). The verdict column is the recommendation for Citequill, not what the tools do: error counts against the score; warning is shown and weighs less; info is shown outside the score; question is put to the owner because only they know the intent. Feasibility: H from raw HTML and headers plus robots.txt and sitemaps; R needs a rendered DOM; P needs PageSpeed Insights; C needs CrUX; G needs Search Console.

### 3.5 On-page tags and content

Google's own positions first, because the tools disagree with them: there is no limit on title length, titles are truncated to fit the device and rewritten when half-empty, obsolete, boilerplate or in the wrong language (A). There is no limit on description length; it is not used in ranking and is used for the snippet when it describes the page better than the text (A). Any number of H1s is fine, "your site is going to rank perfectly fine with no H1 tags or with five" (B, Mueller); heading order does not matter for ranking (A). There is "no magical word count target, minimum or maximum" (A). Keyword density and text-to-HTML ratio are not factors (B, Mueller). Keywords in the URL "have hardly any effect beyond appearing in breadcrumbs" (A).

Two design notes. Let the owner mark an image or a selector as decorative in Citequill and record it in the report, because "whether to treat an image as decorative or informative is a judgment that only the author can make" (A). Never show a decorative image as a problem; show the count of decorative images as a pass, so the owner sees the audit understood them.

Reporting. For a developer: the page URL, the image `src`, the alt value as found (absent, empty and whitespace told apart), a CSS selector, a short HTML snippet, and the ancestor that decided the verdict (the link, the figure). For a non-developer: a thumbnail, the page, the position in words ("third image in the main content", "inside the link to /pricing"), the current alt in quotes, one sentence why, one alt they can paste. Group by image, not by page, because a logo or icon repeats across the site and is fixed once. Rendered DOM beats source HTML: the Semrush and Ahrefs false positives in forums came from markup a plugin injected (D).

### 3.7 Structured data, Open Graph

JSON-LD is Google's recommended format; it may sit in head or body and may be injected by JavaScript (A). The policies: mark up only visible content, match the page, the most specific type, images crawlable; violations draw a manual action that removes rich results but not ranking (A). Google's AI optimisation guide says no special markup is needed for AI features and that structured data should match the visible text (A).

Rich results Google shows in October 2026, 25 types: Article, Breadcrumb (desktop only since January 2025), Carousel, Course list, Dataset (Dataset Search only), Discussion forum, Education Q&A, Employer aggregate rating, Event, Image metadata, Job posting, Local business, Math solver, Movie, Organization, Product, Profile page, Q&A, Recipe, Review snippet, Software app, Speakable, Subscription and paywalled content, Vacation rental, Video (A). Dropped: HowTo (September 2023), FAQ (restricted September 2023, gone 7 May 2026, docs removed June 2026), sitelinks search box (November 2024), special announcement, course info, estimated salary, learning video, vehicle listing, ClaimReview (2025), practice problems (January 2026) (A). Markup for a dropped feature causes no error and brings no result.

There is no API for the Rich Results Test or the schema.org validator (A by absence). `schema-dts` is types only; `structured-data-testing-tool` has had no release since March 2020 (C). So the check is in-house: parse, check `@context` and `@type`, check the required and recommended properties against Google's per-type lists (Article, BreadcrumbList, Organization, LocalBusiness, Product and merchant listing, Event, JobPosting, VideoObject, Review and AggregateRating, Recipe, SoftwareApplication, ProfilePage, Course list, all documented, A), check that image URLs return 200, and compare name, headline, price and rating with the visible text.

### 3.11 AI and GEO

Google-Extended is a robots.txt token with no user agent that controls Gemini training and grounding and has no effect on Search; AI Overviews are served from the normal index (A). The vendors' own documentation separates training agents (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, Meta-ExternalAgent, Applebot-Extended), search agents (OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, PerplexityBot) and user-fetch agents (ChatGPT-User, Claude-User, Perplexity-User), which "generally ignore robots.txt" (C). Blocking a training agent costs no visibility; blocking a search agent removes the site from that assistant's answers. `nosnippet` keeps a page out of AI Overviews and AI Mode as direct input (A). Since July 2025 Cloudflare blocks AI crawlers by default for new customers, and a further default from 15 September 2026 blocks mixed-use crawlers on ad pages for new and free zones (B, C), so a customer's content can be unreadable to assistants through a CDN setting the owner never chose.

## 4. The score

### How the tools do it

| Tool            | Formula                                                                                                                 | Consequence                                                                        | Grade |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----- |
| Ahrefs          | Share of internal URLs with no error-level issue; warnings and notices count for nothing                                 | A missing description on every page, an error in Ahrefs, scores 0                  | A     |
| Semrush         | 0 to 100% from errors and warnings "in relation to the number of performed checks"; errors weigh more; no public formula | Not explainable                                                                    | A     |
| Sitebulb        | From "the amount, type, and priority" of hints; no public weights                                                        | Not explainable                                                                    | A     |
| Screaming Frog  | No score                                                                                                                |                                                                                    | A     |
| Lighthouse SEO  | 10 binary audits; `is-crawlable` is 31% of the weight, each other audit 7.7%; one URL, not a site                       | Fails the category alone on noindex; gives 7.7% to the meta description, which Google does not rank on | A |

Lighthouse's SEO category still exists in version 13.5.0 (September 2026) and in the PageSpeed Insights API (A, `default-config.js`). The removal the owner may remember is the PWA category in 12.0, and the font size, viewport, plugins and tap target audits leaving SEO in 12.0 and 13.0 (A).

### Recommended formula

100 minus penalties, each penalty scaled by the share of pages that fail its check, severity 3 for a Google-documented requirement, 2 for a Google-documented recommendation, 1 for a tool convention, and a gate: a decisive failure on the home page or on more than 10% of pages caps the score at 49, Lighthouse's "poor" band. Checks with no data (no field Core Web Vitals, no hreflang, no structured data) leave the denominator. The formula is published in `docs/` so the owner can see why the number is what it is, which no tool above does.

| Area                                                                                        | Weight       | Why                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Indexability and crawlability: status, noindex, robots, canonical, sitemap, redirects, orphans | 40           | The only checks Google calls requirements; a failure removes the page from the index |
| HTTPS and security basics                                                                   | 10           | A ranking signal, if lightweight; browsers block mixed content                       |
| Mobile: viewport, same content                                                              | 5            | Mobile-first indexing; a one-line fix                                                |
| On-page essentials: title present and unique, description present, alt text, H1 present     | 20           | Where Google says to use the words people search for; presence and uniqueness, never length bands |
| Internal links and anchor text: broken, generic, uncrawlable, depth                         | 15           | How Google finds pages                                                               |
| Performance: field vitals when present, else response time and compression                  | 10           | Used by ranking systems, "no single signal"                                          |
| Structured data, hreflang, AI access                                                        | 0, conditional | Penalise invalid markup or a blocked search agent only when the thing exists        |

Decisive checks, each a cap: a page meant to rank returns 200 and is not a soft 404; it carries no noindex and is not disallowed, nor are its CSS and JavaScript; its canonical, if any, points to an indexable 200 on the same site; the sitemap lists only indexable canonical 200 URLs; redirect chains are under 10 hops with no loops; HTML under 2 MB; HTTPS with no active mixed content; a viewport; real `<a href>` links with every page reachable; no spam pattern.

Noise, reported as hints outside the score: title and description length bands, pixel widths, word count, text-to-HTML ratio, H1 count, heading order, underscores, URL length, security headers other than HTTPS, `llms.txt`, accessibility rules beyond alt and link names.

## 5. What a first version ships

1. A new run trigger `audit`, started from an Audit section and later by a monthly cron. Step one reads robots.txt and every sitemap and seeds a queue in Postgres; each step takes 50 URLs, fetches at 2 a second with the named user agent and a per-site secret header, parses, runs the checks, writes the page row and failing findings, and returns new URLs; the last step cross-checks sitemap and crawl sets, fetches the home page as each search agent, calls PageSpeed Insights on up to 20 pages and CrUX at origin level, and scores.
2. The checks of sections 3.1 to 3.4, 3.6 and 3.9, which are all feasible from raw HTML, plus the JSON-LD validator of 3.7 and the robots checks of 3.11. About 40 checks with a Google source, then the rest.
3. A report grouped by finding, each with the pages affected, and for images the fields of section 3.6. Every finding links to the Google page it stands on.
4. The owner can mark a finding as intended (a decorative image, a noindex they meant, a cross-domain canonical), and the score counts it as a pass.
5. Rendering, Search Console joins and the mobile user agent diff in a second version.

## What the owner decided

The grilling of 17 October 2026 (Q5 to Q49) answered the open questions and changed six recommendations above. The behaviour is in `../audit.md` and the reasons in `../decisions.md`; this section only lists where the decisions differ from the research:

- The audit is a build step before publishing, not after it, and runs on demand only; the monthly run above was not taken.
- The full catalogue ships, in slices on `main` in the order of the weights, not a first version of 40 checks.
- 5 requests a second to the host, not 2; a 5 000 page site takes about 17 minutes of fetching plus the resource checks.
- AI access is an area of its own with weight 10, taken from indexability and on-page; the weights are 35, 15, 15, 10, 5, 10, 10.
- Rendering from the start: a sample in a Vercel Sandbox with Citequill's own Playwright image, and every page when the raw HTML is a shell. Chromium in a function and hosted browsers were rejected.
- PageSpeed Insights and CrUX wait for the Search Console step; performance in the first version is the audit's own response time and compression.
- robots.txt is obeyed by default as the `Googlebot` group, with a per-project switch; a `CitequillBot` group is honoured always.
- Only the snippet and selector of a failing element are stored; no page HTML, no 90 day storage.
- Every locale is audited and the score is one per site, with a language filter in the report.
- Title width in the result font counts at severity 1; the other tool conventions are hints with "Google does not count this".
- Costs are measured per audit, added to the run's cost and shown to the operator alone.

A review of the documents on the same day corrected facts in this research: Cloudflare's 1 July 2025 change blocks AI training crawlers by default for new domains, and from 15 September 2026 new domains block the Training and Agent categories on pages with ads while Search stays allowed, with mixed-use crawlers getting the strictest rule (the "free zones" wording in section 3.11 came from third-party coverage); the Princeton GEO paper was measured on a simulated engine and on Perplexity with uploaded files, not on live web retrieval; `nocache` limits Bing's Copilot to URL, title and snippet where `noarchive` removes the page; Claude-User honours robots.txt where ChatGPT-User and Perplexity-User may not; a Workflow step runs 800 seconds and the 1 800 second beta does not apply to the generated step routes; sandbox memory is $0.0212 per GB-hour, twice the function rate in the table above, and rendering all 5 000 pages costs about $0.90, with snapshots avoided by `persistent: false`. The behaviour that follows is in `../audit.md`.
