# Audit

The audit reads every page of the customer's site, checks each one against a fixed catalogue and gives the site a score from 0 to 100. It runs on demand from the Audit section, never on a schedule. It calls no model: every finding has a fixed explanation, a sentence on what Google says and a link to the Google page it stands on. The score has a published formula, this document, so the owner can work out by hand why the number is what it is. The research behind the catalogue is in `research/2026-10-17-seo-audit.md`; the decisions are in [`decisions.md`](decisions.md).

Two readers use the audit. The owner of a site reads what to fix. Citequill reads which pages exist and which of them a page it writes may link to.

## Scope

- Up to 5 000 URLs per audit, in every language of the site. A site with more pages gets an audit of the first 5 000 in discovery order, and the report says so in its header.
- Every page is read as raw HTML, because most crawlers and every AI fetcher read raw HTML. Once the render module ships, a sample is rendered in a browser as well, because Google renders, and a difference between the two is a finding, and a site whose raw HTML is an application shell has every page rendered. Until then the report says when a site's raw HTML is a shell, as the Rendering section describes.
- One audit per project at a time. A second click while one runs opens the running one. A click on a stalled audit cancels its Workflow, ends it as `failed` with the message "The audit made no progress for 15 minutes." and then starts a new one.
- An audit that writes no progress for 15 minutes counts as stalled, the rule of every run. Stop ends it as `failed` with the message "You stopped the audit." and a partial score from the pages read so far, without the checks that compare pages with each other, and without the checks on robots.txt and the sitemaps when it stopped before reading them.
- What an audit costs Citequill is measured and stored, and shown to the operator alone. The customer sees pages, time and findings.

## The score

### Formula

Every check in the catalogue has a severity, an area and a set of pages it applies to, named at the top of each catalogue table. A site-level check, such as a missing sitemap, applies to the site and fails or passes as one. A check marked "rendered" or "sample" applies to the pages of the rendered sample.

The failing share of a check is the pages that fail it divided by the pages it applies to, after findings the owner marked as intended are counted as passes. A site-level check has a share of 1 or 0. A page fails a check once, however many times the check fails on it: ten images without alt on one page are one failing page, and the report shows the ten.

```
check penalty = area weight × failing share × (severity / 3)
area penalty  = min(area weight, Σ check penalties of the area)
score         = round(100 − Σ area penalties)
```

So a severity 3 check failing on every page costs its area's whole weight, a severity 2 check failing on every page costs two thirds of it, and two common faults in one area cannot cost more than the area. A check with no data, because the site has no hreflang, no structured data or no images, adds nothing; an area with no data at all costs nothing.

Then the gate: when a gate check fails on the home page, or on more than 10% of the pages it applies to, pages with a noindex of the owner's own left out, the score is capped at 49. A failing site-level gate check caps the score by itself. A mark of intended lifts the gate like any other pass.

### Areas and weights

| Area         | Weight | What is in it                                                                                                       |
| ------------ | ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Indexability | 35     | robots.txt, noindex, status codes, redirects, canonicals, duplicates, sitemaps, hreflang, spam patterns, the shell  |
| On-page      | 15     | title, description, headings, the head, images and alt text, structured data, Open Graph                            |
| Links        | 15     | broken links and resources, uncrawlable links, orphans, anchor text, links that exist only after JavaScript         |
| HTTPS        | 10     | HTTP, certificates, mixed content, forms over HTTP                                                                  |
| Mobile       | 5      | the viewport, interstitials, content that differs on a phone                                                        |
| Performance  | 10     | response time and compression of the HTML, measured by the audit's own fetch                                        |
| AI access    | 10     | search agents of AI assistants in robots.txt and at the CDN, snippet and archive controls, the shape Bing documents |

Structured data and hreflang have no area of their own: invalid markup costs on-page points and a broken hreflang set costs indexability points, both only when the site has them. Indexability, main content in the raw HTML and working links matter to AI assistants too, but they count once, in their own areas.

### Severity

| Severity | Meaning                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 3        | Google documents that the page, or a whole mechanism of the site, stops working: the page is not indexed, not shown or demoted, or a sitemap, an hreflang set or a canonical is ignored. A spam policy. Only a 3 can be a gate |
| 2        | Google documents it as a recommendation, or as the cause of a lost feature or a fault a reader meets: a rewritten title, a lost rich result, a broken image, a form over HTTP                                                  |
| 1        | a convention with a visible effect Google or Bing documents: a title cut off in the results, Bing's advice on the shape of a page                                                                                              |
| hint     | shown in the report with the sentence "Google does not count this", or in AI access "Citequill does not count this in the score", outside the score                                                                            |
| question | shown as a question to the owner, because only they know the intent: a content page with noindex, a cross-domain canonical, a sitemap listing URLs outside its folder or host                                                  |

The report names them Critical, Major, Minor, Hint and Question, never by the digit alone.

The gate checks are the ones Google names as technical requirements, the spam policies, and HTTPS: `robots-googlebot-blocked`, `noindex-site`, `soft-404`, `page-5xx`, `redirect-loop`, `redirect-chain-long`, `spam-pattern`, `https-missing`, `mixed-active`. A recommendation never caps the score, however common its failure, because one template element (a menu item without `href`) would otherwise cap a whole site.

### Bands

| Score     | Band       | Colour |
| --------- | ---------- | ------ |
| 90 to 100 | good       | green  |
| 50 to 89  | needs work | amber  |
| 0 to 49   | poor       | red    |

### Intended

The owner marks a finding as intended at one of three grains: this URL, this selector on every page (the same logo on 300 pages), or the whole check for the project. The selector grain is offered when the selector has an id or a class, or when a mark on that selector already exists; a bare tag such as `a` or `img` names no element of the template and a mark on it would cover every such element on the site, so the card offers no new mark on one, and keeps the button of an existing mark so the owner can remove it. A marked finding counts as a pass in the score and stays in the report, labelled intended, so the owner sees the audit understood the choice. The marks live in the project: a click recomputes the score and the counters of the latest audit at once, with the code every audit scores with, and a database function writes them; every later audit applies the marks itself; older audits keep the score they had, because the function writes only to the latest. This holds for the AI access area too: an owner who blocks OAI-SearchBot on purpose marks it, and the score respects the choice.

### While the catalogue is incomplete

The catalogue ships in slices. A check that has not shipped is a check with no data, so the score is computed from the checks that exist, and the score header says how many checks it stands on: "scored from 48 of 112 checks". The comparison with a previous audit stands on the checks both audits ran, as the report section says, so a check that shipped between them, or a CDN test that ran in one of them only, changes neither the difference nor the counts.

### Hints

A hint is shown and never scored. Four kinds:

- a tool convention Google says it does not count: description length, H1 count, word count, URL length, heading order, text-to-HTML ratio, keyword density
- a GEO hint from a single or contradicted study, with its evidence labelled
- an accessibility rule beyond alt text, link names and the page language, because accessibility is not a ranking factor
- a thing the audit could not verify, such as an external link whose host refused the audit, so the owner checks it by hand

Title length is the one tool convention in the score, at severity 1, because Google cuts a long title in the results and rarely rewrites a good one. The threshold is a width of 600 px in the font of Google's result titles, Citequill's threshold rather than Google's, because "Display" and "iiiiiii" have the same count of characters and a different width.

## The crawl

### Discovery

1. The home page at the base URL of every locale of the project, at click depth 0, then `robots.txt` and every sitemap it names, then `/sitemap.xml` and `/sitemap_index.xml` when robots.txt names none. Up to 50 sitemap files are read, on the site's own hosts only, a gzipped file unpacked first, known by its first two bytes, because a server may send one as `application/xml`.
2. Every URL of every sitemap, sitemap indexes followed, with `lastmod` and the hreflang, image and video children kept; the hreflang children stay on the URL's queue entry, so the hreflang checks can read them when the page is read and when the site is checked.
3. Then breadth first from the home page by click depth: the links of every fetched page join the queue, on the site's own hosts, every locale included. The site's own hosts are the hosts of the project's locales, each with its `www` twin or its bare twin. The queue lives in the database, not in the Workflow's step arguments. It is read one depth at a time, so a page's click depth is the shortest path from a home page. A URL that only a sitemap lists has no click depth and is read after every URL a click reaches. The target of a redirect joins the queue at the depth of the URL that redirected. The hreflang alternates of every URL that answered 200, from its tags when the answer is HTML and from its `Link` header whatever the answer is, and of every sitemap entry join the queue when they sit on the site's own hosts, without a click depth and without counting as a link, because Google asks every alternate to be indexable and the audit can only say so about a page it read; an alternate on another host gets no request, because the audit reads only the site. Each page records its click depth, the count of pages linking to it and the first 5 pages that link to it, which is what the orphan, depth and broken-link checks read; the audit keeps no fuller list of who links to whom. The queue keeps the count and the 5 pages while the audit runs, and the page's row in `site_pages` takes the count before the site checks run.
4. URLs with query parameters are read up to 50 per path, enough to say "this path has indexable facets" without spending the budget on them. Fragments are dropped.
5. The queue stops at 5 000 URLs, blocked ones included. When a URL is refused because the queue is full, the audit is partial.

### Rate and manners

- 5 requests a second to the site, fixed, every host of the site together, with up to 5 requests in flight. A request waits for its turn before its 15 seconds start, and each further hop of a redirect takes a turn of its own. A page whose 200 HTML answer took over 800 ms is read a second time, for `html-slow`, at the same pace. On 429 or 503 the audit waits for `Retry-After`, or doubles its pause up to a minute, and goes on; a `Retry-After` longer than a minute is cut to a minute. The URL is asked again up to 3 times and then recorded with the answer it got. Twenty 429, 5xx or time-out answers in a row end the audit as `failed` with the message that the site stopped answering or rate-limited the audit, because carrying on would hit a server that is down. A request that gets no connection counts as a time-out. A request that fails on the certificate or on the TLS version is an answer, `tls-invalid`, never `page-5xx`, and does not count in the twenty, so the audit of a site with a broken certificate runs to the end and says what is wrong. The count and the pause carry over from one step to the next.
- A Workflow step runs for at most 300 seconds on the Hobby plan, so each step takes 50 URLs, has a wall budget of 240 seconds, and returns what it did not read to the queue. On the Pro plan the limit is 800 seconds and the budget 600.
- Every request carries the user agent `CitequillBot/1.0 (+https://citequill.com/bot)`. Two kinds of request carry another user agent with the same `CitequillBot/1.0 (+https://citequill.com/bot)` appended, so the bot is still named in every request: the phone fetch of the sample, with the user agent of Chrome on an Android phone and the header `sec-ch-ua-mobile: ?1`, so a site that serves a phone template by user agent serves it, and the CDN test below. The audit reads no page as Googlebot, because a CDN verifies Googlebot by its addresses and blocks a copy; two control requests of the CDN test claim to be Googlebot and Bingbot, with the bot's name appended, to learn whether the CDN does. The page at `citequill.com/bot` says what the bot does, how to block it in robots.txt and how to let it through a firewall.
- Every request to the site's own hosts, over https and http alike, carries the project's bot secret in the header `citequill-bot-secret`, the renderer's requests included; the header never goes to a foreign host or on a redirect hop off the site. Header names are case-insensitive on the wire, so the lowercase form is what a firewall rule matches. The owner pastes the secret into a WAF skip rule in Cloudflare, as an expression Settings shows with the secret in it, or into a bypass rule in the Vercel firewall, because function IP addresses change and a user agent is spoofable. Cloudflare's Free plan has Bot Fight Mode, which no rule bypasses, and Vercel's Attack Mode runs before custom rules, so no rule bypasses it either; the owner turns either off for the audit. The secret is generated per project, shown in Settings with the two instructions and one sentence saying it is sent over http too, and can be regenerated there. The requests of the CDN test below are the ones that leave the header out.
- Each request to the site is one guarded fetch, the crawl's `fetchPublic()` with headers, HEAD and 10 hops added: public hosts only, redirects by hand with the hop chain recorded, 15 seconds, the body cut at 2 MB, which is where Googlebot stops too. A sitemap is cut at 50 MB instead, the protocol's limit, counted after a gzipped file is unpacked, and a sitemap whose answer stops partway, at the time limit or because the server closed the connection, keeps what was read, as a cut file.

### Probes

After the last page is read and before the site checks, one step sends the requests that no page gives: a random missing path on the https origin of every host of the site and under every base URL of a locale, with GET, judged by the end of its redirect chain; the home page and 20 indexable paths of the sample under each of their host variants, with GET, so the variant's canonical is read, and, for an https URL, under `http:`, which `https-missing` judges and `host-variants` leaves alone, so one missing redirect is one finding; the same home page and 20 paths once more as the desktop fetch and once with the phone user agent, back to back, which `mobile-content-differs` compares, because the Read step keeps no extract to compare with; and one GET to the first linked URL of each host the pages link to that sits under the site's own domain and looks like a staging host. The step starts with the CDN test below, so the other probes do not spend the budget before it, and asks the home page of the main locale for markdown for `markdown-alternate`: first by `Accept`, then its two markdown twins and `/llms.txt`, stopping at the first way found, so it sends 4 requests at most. The probes go at the site's pace, carry the bot secret on the site's own hosts only, count in the audit's requests, and a probe the budget leaves unsent gives no finding. A probe URL that robots.txt blocks for the audit, by the rule the Read step uses, is not sent, because a `CitequillBot` group that disallows everything leaves the audit only robots.txt and the sitemaps, as the bot page says. The same step reads the queue for the content paths of `robots-googlebot-blocked`, which needs no request. A stopped audit sends no probe, so the probe checks count as not run.

### robots.txt

- By default the audit obeys robots.txt as Googlebot does: the `Googlebot` group when there is one, else `*`. A disallowed URL is recorded as blocked, not fetched, and reported. Matching follows Google: the longest rule wins, and an allow wins a tie. A switch per project turns this off, so the audit reads what robots.txt hides, and the choice is recorded; the switch is not yet in Settings, so every audit obeys. Two checks, `noindex-blocked` and the noindex half of `sitemap-url-error`, need the content of a disallowed page and run only when the switch is off.
- A `CitequillBot` group, when the owner wrote one, has its rules honoured always, because it is the owner's explicit word to this bot. With the switch on, a URL is blocked when either the Googlebot rules or the `CitequillBot` group disallow it; with the switch off, only the `CitequillBot` group blocks. `Crawl-delay` is ignored in every group, the `CitequillBot` group included, as Google ignores it; it shows up only through the `robots-unsupported` hint about the site's own file.
- The rules for AI agents are read only to report them.
- robots.txt is read once per audit, from every origin the audit may fetch: each of the site's own hosts, over https and over http, because a robots.txt governs only its own host and scheme, and a site on several domains has one on each. A 4xx means no restrictions. A 5xx or a time-out is a finding and the audit treats the site as fully allowed, so the owner sees the pages; Google stops crawling for 12 hours, then uses its cached copy for 30 days, or crawls freely when it has none. The checks on the file itself read the robots.txt of the origins the home pages are on; the files of the other origins decide only which URLs are blocked, because a twin host with nothing on it gives no answer at all.

### Resources and external links

- The Read step collects every resource a page links to into a frontier of its own, `audit_resources`, with the first 5 pages linking to it: the internal images of every page, the `og:image` of every page, the image URLs of the JSON-LD of every indexable page, the scripts and stylesheets of every page, and the external links of every page. The scripts and stylesheets are checked only for the pages of the 20-path sample, because the Read step cannot know the sample yet; a script or stylesheet none of whose 5 recorded pages is in the sample is marked checked without a request. A URL a page uses in two ways is queued once, as a JSON-LD image first, then as an `og:image`, an image, a script or stylesheet and a link, and keeps the kind it was first queued with, so a broken image that one page uses in its JSON-LD and another as its `og:image` is reported as a JSON-LD image on both. An image, `og:image`, JSON-LD image, script or stylesheet is internal when its host is one of the site's own hosts or sits under the project's domain, as `cdn.acme.com` does under `acme.com`. A link is internal when its host is one of the site's own hosts, the ones the queue reads; a link to any other host, one under the project's domain included, is external.
- After the last page is read, a step of its own checks the frontier in batches of 50 URLs like the pages, with the same wall budget, each URL once with a HEAD request, GET when HEAD answers 405 or 501, because a broken image sits on one page and the report names it. Internal resources go at the site's pace, with the bot secret on the site's own hosts only. External links, and any `og:image`, JSON-LD image, script or stylesheet on a foreign host, go at 2 requests a second per foreign host, one request in flight per host, with one retry after no answer, and without the bot secret. The audit reads no robots.txt of a foreign host: a HEAD to a public URL is what every link checker sends, and the bot page tells the host's administrator how to refuse it.
- A 403 or 429 from a foreign host is `link-external-refused`, a hint, never a broken link, and the rest of that host's URLs in the audit get the same hint without a request. A finding of a broken or refused resource sits on each of the 5 pages recorded as linking to it, with the total count of linking pages in its detail.
- The frontier takes 2 000 distinct external links and 5 000 distinct images, `og:image`s, scripts and stylesheets per audit, the first seen first. A page whose resources were not all checked still counts as checked, so a cap never makes the audit partial. The `Content-Length` of an image's answer is what `img-heavy` reads.

### The CDN test

The home page of the main locale is fetched without the bot secret, first four times as a control, then once as each of 8 AI search agents, then once more as the browser control. The controls, in order, are Chrome on a desktop, `CitequillBot` alone, Googlebot and Bingbot, each but `CitequillBot` with `CitequillBot/1.0 (+https://citequill.com/bot)` appended. The agents are those of `ai-search-bots-blocked` but Bingbot and Google-Extended, with the same name appended, so the bot is named in every request. An agent's user agent is the one its vendor prints, with the browser control's Chrome version where the vendor writes `W.X.Y.Z`; Anthropic prints none for Claude-SearchBot, so the test sends its token alone, which a rule matching the token meets as the real agent does. Bingbot is a control and not an agent, because Cloudflare's managed rule against fake Bingbots refuses a request from another address exactly as a block by name would; Google-Extended is a robots.txt token with no user agent of its own.

A control passes when its final answer is a 200, and fails on any other answer but a 5xx; a 429 is a refusal, because Vercel's Security Checkpoint answers a browser with one. Each control rules out one cause that refuses Citequill's requests while the real agents get through:

- the browser control: the site refuses automated traffic from Citequill's address, as Bot Fight Mode, DataDome or the data centre rule of AWS Bot Control do; the outcome is `browser-refused`
- `CitequillBot`: the site refuses every client that is not a browser, as Browser Integrity Check or Vercel's Bot Protection do; `bot-refused`
- Googlebot and Bingbot: the site checks that a request claiming to be a known crawler comes from that crawler's addresses, as Cloudflare's Bot Management on openai.com, canva.com and pracuj.pl, and the CDNs of Wikipedia, CNN and Reddit did on 19 October 2026, for a claimed Googlebot, Bingbot and AI agent alike; `spoof-refused`

When a control fails, the test says nothing about the real agents: the controls after it and the agents are not asked, and `ai-cdn-blocked` and `pay-per-crawl` count as not run. A control that gets a 5xx or no answer at all, a time-out or a TLS failure, refuses nothing, so the test stops there with no outcome. The browser control asked again after the agents must get a 200 too, because a rate limit that starts partway through the test refuses the last agents as a rule by name would; when it does not, the test has no outcome and gives no finding. When all five control requests pass, an agent whose final answer is a 4xx other than 429 is refused by a rule that names it, because the site let a browser, a neutral bot and a claimed Googlebot and Bingbot through, and sites refuse an agent with 403, 406 or a code of their own, as nature.com answered PerplexityBot 406 and spiegel.de answered Claude-SearchBot 471. A 402 with a `crawler-price` header is `pay-per-crawl` instead. An agent's 429, 5xx or time-out gives no finding, with `cf-mitigated: challenge` or without, because Shopify's edge answers a rate limit with a 429 challenge. An agent that robots.txt disallows on the home page, by the rules `ai-search-bots-blocked` reads, is left out of both findings, because that check already reports the owner's choice; its answer stays in the detail. The audit records the outcome on the audit row: the test counts as run only when all five control requests passed and every agent was asked, so a stopped audit, a budget that ran out or a home page that robots.txt blocks for the audit leaves both checks not run.

The wording says what was done: "fetched from Citequill's address as OAI-SearchBot, the home page answered 403, while a browser, before and after the agents, a neutral bot and a claimed Googlebot and Bingbot got 200". It says that a CDN which verifies bots by their address may let the real agent through, so the owner checks the CDN's logs, and it names the setting by the CDN the answer's headers show: the firewall's rules on Vercel (`x-vercel-id`, even behind Cloudflare, because only Vercel adds it), else the Search and Agent settings of AI Crawl Control on Cloudflare (`cf-ray`), else the bot settings of the host or CDN. A rare setup still misleads the test, a rule that checks the addresses of AI agents but not of Googlebot and Bingbot, as a Fastly customer may write one, and the owner marks such a finding intended. The requests take their turns at the site's pace, are not retried, take no pause from a 429 or 503, do not count in the twenty failures in a row, and count only in the audit's requests, not in its 429 and 5xx counts. The bot page says the audit does this.

## Rendering

The render module waits for a site in use that is a shell, as the Delivery section says, so the sample is not rendered yet and the rendered and sample checks count as not run. The shell test below runs today, on the raw HTML, and the report says when a site is a shell.

### The shell test

A page is a shell when its raw HTML has under 200 characters of text in the body once the elements the main content leaves out are left out: scripts, styles, `noscript`, `template`, `svg`, `iframe`, navigation, `header`, `footer`, `aside`, forms, dialogs and hidden elements. The test reads the page with the content React streams put in place, as the paragraph on streaming under the catalogue says, so a page that streams its content is no shell. A body that holds only an empty root element such as `#root`, `#__next` or `#app` has no text, so it is a shell by the same rule. The Read step runs the test on every page that answers 200 with HTML read whole, and stores the answer on the page; a page cut at 2 MB is no shell, because the cut can fall before the body. The site is a shell when at least one home page answered 200, every home page that answered 200 is a shell, and over half of the sample is.

Until the render module ships, the card of a finished audit of a site that is a shell says so: Citequill read almost no text in the raw HTML, Google renders JavaScript and some hosts send crawlers a prerendered copy, so the findings about the content may not hold for them. The sentence does not change the score. Once the module ships, a site that is a shell has every page it read rendered, on 10 sandboxes of 2 vCPU each. That needs the Pro plan: on Hobby, 10 sandboxes are the whole limit of sandboxes at once, 20 vCPUs the whole allowance of the first minute, and the render takes about 4 to 8 CPU-hours of the 5 a month included, and going over any quota stops sandboxes for 30 days.

### The sample

Of the pages that answered 200, the home page, every top-level page up to 10, then one page per URL pattern until 50 pages. A pattern is the path with every segment that looks like an identifier (digits, UUIDs, dates) replaced by a placeholder, grouped by its first segment and its depth. Differences between raw and rendered HTML are a feature of the template, so one page per pattern finds them all.

The sample is fetched again, raw, with a phone's user agent. That fetch is the raw side of every raw-versus-rendered comparison, so a site that serves a phone template is not reported as "links appear only after JavaScript", and it is the phone side of the checks that compare phone and desktop content. Until the render module ships, the probe step sends the phone fetch for the home page and the 20 indexable paths of the variant sample, so `mobile-content-differs` runs on them.

### The engine

Rendering runs in a Vercel Sandbox from Citequill's own image: a Dockerfile in `sandbox/render/` with Playwright's Chromium, built and pushed by the owner, as the owner pushes migrations, with `vercel link`, `vercel vcr login docker` and `vercel vcr build docker sandbox/render render:latest --push`. The build needs Docker, Podman or Buildah on the owner's machine and a `linux/amd64` image; the repository belongs to the Vercel project that creates the sandboxes, and `Sandbox.create()` answers `image_not_ready` until the registry shows the image as ready. Custom images shipped as a public beta on 30 June 2026. A sandbox runs neither `ENTRYPOINT` nor `CMD`, so the step starts the script with `runCommand()`. A Workflow step mints a token for this audit, stores its hash on the audit with an expiry, creates the sandboxes with the URLs and the token, sets their timeout and turns persistence off, and starts the render script detached. The script posts each page's result to an API route of Citequill, outside the sign-in proxy, that checks the token, that the audit is still running and that the URL is in the render set, and accepts a repeated post for one URL once; the sandbox never holds the Supabase secret key. The workflow waits on a hook that the route resumes when a sandbox reports done, then a step ends the sandboxes and clears the token; Stop does the same. Playwright sends the bot secret and the Googlebot Smartphone user agent, so the CDN treats the renderer as it treats the crawler.

Renderer settings, Google's documented ones where they exist and Screaming Frog's where Google is silent: Chromium, viewport 411 by 731, the Googlebot Smartphone user agent, no interaction, wait for `load` and then up to 5 seconds of network quiet, a hard cap of 10 seconds per page, media and fonts blocked.

### What is compared

Title, meta description, meta robots, canonical, H1, the `<a href>` set, the `<img src>` set, the JSON-LD blocks and the length of the main content, rendered against the phone fetch of the same page. Each difference is a finding: "canonical present only after JavaScript", "noindex only in the raw HTML", "47 links appear only after JavaScript".

## The catalogue

Each check has an identifier the findings, the marks and the report use. Severity 3, 2 and 1 count in the score; hint and question do not. "Gate" marks a check that caps the score at 49 as the formula says. "Rendered" marks a check that needs the rendered DOM and applies to the sample; "sample" marks a check that needs the sample's second fetch. The raw pass reads attributes and inline styles only; a rule that needs computed styles or layout is marked rendered. The catalogue has 179 rows, 112 of them scored.

Before any check reads a page, the audit puts the content React streams where React's own script puts it, because the text is in the raw HTML and Google, which renders, reads it there. A page that streams a Suspense boundary sends its fallback first, then the content in a hidden element whose id starts with `S:`, then an inline script that calls a function to move it. The audit applies the calls of the page's inline scripts in their order: `$RS("S:x", "P:x")` puts the children of the element with id `S:x` where the element with id `P:x` stands and removes both; `$RC("B:x", "S:x")`, and `$RR` with the same first two arguments, removes the boundary's fallback, from the element with id `B:x` up to the comment that closes the boundary, nested boundaries counted, and puts the children of `S:x` there. A call whose element is missing does nothing, and a block that no call moves stays hidden and is left out as every hidden element is. The words of the moved content take the place they were moved to, for every rule that reads where a word stands. Two readers keep the page as it was served: the links and their anchors, because Google takes the links of a page from its raw HTML before it renders, so a link that stands only in a fallback is still found, and the elements the parser moved out of the `head`, because no call moves one and only the page as served shows where they stood.

The main content of a page is its `main` element, else its `[role="main"]` element, else the outermost `article` that holds the page's first H1, even in the `header` of that `article`, as WordPress writes it; else, when that H1 stands outside every `article` and outside the elements left out below, the `article` with the most text if it holds over half of the body's text, and otherwise the body, because the articles are then teasers of other posts; else, when the H1 sits in an element left out below, such as the site's `header`, or the page has none, the `article` with the most text; else the body. Left out of it are scripts, styles, `noscript`, `template`, `svg`, `iframe`, navigation, `header`, `footer`, `aside`, forms, dialogs (`dialog`, `[role="dialog"]`, `[role="alertdialog"]` and `[aria-modal="true"]`, where cookie banners sit) and every element hidden by `hidden`, `aria-hidden="true"` or, inside the body, an inline `display: none` or `visibility: hidden`, written in any case and spacing, because an anti-flicker snippet hides the whole `body` until its script runs. An `article` with no text once these are left out is no candidate. Its text, from which the audit counts words and takes the body hash and the SimHash, has a space at every block element and every `br`, so the last word of a paragraph or a list item never joins the first word of the next; an inline element such as `b` adds none, so a word split by one stays whole. The texts of the headings, and of the hidden blocks the spam check reads, have the same spaces. The crawl reads the same main content for its facts, the streamed content put in place.

A page's alternates come by three methods, which Google calls equivalent: `<link rel="alternate" hreflang>` tags, a `Link` header with `rel="alternate"` and `hreflang`, and the `xhtml:link` children of the page's entry in a sitemap, which the queue keeps. The tags are read from an HTML answer; the header is read from every 200 answer, a PDF included, because Google's own example of the header method is a PDF. A tag whose `rel` also says `stylesheet` is an alternative stylesheet, not a page, and a tag without `href`, or with an empty one, is no alternate; an `href` that does not resolve to a URL is an alternate with a fault, counted by its text, so a page whose only alternates are faulty is in scope. The page's set is the union of the three, and the hreflang checks apply to the indexable pages whose set is not empty; `hreflang-methods` alone compares the methods with each other, as sets of language and absolute URL. `hreflang-return`, `hreflang-target` and `hreflang-target-canonical` read the alternate's own row, so an alternate the audit did not read, because the queue was full or robots.txt kept it out, gives no finding, and `sitemap-hreflang` reads the alternate's own sitemap entry, so an alternate the queue never took gives none either, while an alternate the queue holds with no sitemap entry is a missing return link. A URL and its trailing-slash twin are two URLs, as Google reads them, so a page that lists itself with the slash it does not have does not list itself. A code is checked with Node's `Intl.DisplayNames`, which knows the ISO lists and tells `xx` from `pl`, after a shape test of two letters, an optional four-letter script and an optional two-letter region; `EU`, `UN` and `UK` are refused by name, because Google says they have no effect while ISO lists the first two and `Intl` knows all three. A finding on one alternate names that URL as its subject, with the tag's selector and snippet when it came from the HTML, so the same bad tag on 300 pages is one row of the by-finding view; a finding on the page's whole set, `hreflang-x-default` and `hreflang-methods`, has no subject, and the half of `hreflang-return` and `sitemap-hreflang` that says the page does not list itself has the page's own URL.

The structured data checks read every `<script type="application/ld+json">` block of the raw HTML; a block whose text is blank holds nothing and is no block. A block's top-level nodes are the object it holds, the items of an array and the items of an `@graph`, which take the `@context` of the object that holds them; every object inside them with a `@type` is a nested node. The other checks read only the top-level nodes whose context `jsonld-invalid` accepts, and what they hold, so a block without a schema.org context is one finding and not one per check. A value that is an object with an `@id` is read as the node of the page with that `@id`, in any block, with its own properties kept, as WordPress plugins write their graphs; the node is the top-level one, or the first with a `@type`, when two objects share the `@id`. A top-level `Review` or `AggregateRating` that another node's `review` or `aggregateRating` points to is read as nested, so it needs no `itemReviewed`, as Google says. A property is present when its value is not empty. A dotted path such as `offers.price` is present when any value along an array has it, and is not reported while the path above it is listed for the feature and missing, so a missing `offers` is one missing property and not three. Where Google accepts one of several properties, such as `jobLocation` or `applicantLocationRequirements`, or `aggregateRating` or `review`, any one of them is enough. The last item of a breadcrumb trail needs no `item`, as Google says, so a trail of one item lacks no `item`. The required and recommended properties are Google's, read from the page of each feature in the gallery on 19 October 2026 and recorded in `docs/research/2026-10-19-structured-data.md`, with the link to that page in the finding's detail. Each type of a node takes the feature of the nearest type in its ancestry that a feature names, so a `Restaurant` is read as a `LocalBusiness` and not as an `Organization`, and a `BlogPosting` as an `Article` and not as a forum post. A feature another type of the same node descends from is dropped, and the missing properties of the features left join the node's one finding. A `Product` that is a merchant listing, as `merchant-price-zero` reads it, takes the merchant listing's lists, and any other `Product` the product snippet's; an `AggregateOffer`'s `lowPrice` counts as the price in both, so a merchant listing with one is `merchant-price-zero` and not a missing price too. Left out: the properties a page says apply in one case only, such as a rescheduled event, a remote job or a restaurant; the registry identifiers of an `Organization`, `duns`, `globalLocationNumber`, `iso6523Code`, `leiCode`, `naics`, `taxID` and `vatID`, which depend on the country; the policies an organization or an offer nests, returns, loyalty and shipping, which a shop opts into and which Merchant Center can set instead; the features whose type serves other uses and which a page opts into, image license metadata on `ImageObject`, carousels on `ItemList`, speakable and paywalled content; the features Google keeps for partners or feeds, vacation rentals and book actions; and the nodes `jsonld-dropped-feature` names. The vocabulary is schema.org's V30.1 release of 16 September 2026, the pending types included, kept in the code and refreshed by hand.

`jsonld-mismatch` folds both sides before it compares: the character references of the markup, such as `&amp;` and `&#8217;`, decoded, then Unicode NFKC, lower case, and every character other than a letter or a digit dropped, so "Air Max 90 – Black" matches "Air Max 90 Black". The page's visible text, which `lorem-ipsum` and `text-ratio` read too, puts a space between the texts of two elements, so a heading and the price after it stay two numbers in minified HTML. A price matches a number in the text whose digits, once the grouping marks between them are dropped (a space of any width, `.`, `,`, `'` or `’`), equal the price's digits with two decimals, or without them when the decimals are zero: 1299.00 matches "1 299 zł", "1.299,00 €" and "1299". A number that follows another with only a space between is read on its own too, so "Air Max 90 129,00 zł" holds 129.00. A price that does not read as a number, or reads as 0, is not compared, because a page writes a free offer as a word. The text is the raw HTML's, so a price a script writes after load reads as missing until the render module compares the rendered page. A rating is not compared, because stars are drawn rather than written.

A structured data finding names the node's `@id` as its subject, else its type, with the block's selector and snippet, so two nodes of one type without an `@id` on one page are one finding. A `jsonld-invalid` finding about a block that does not parse names the block alone, and a `jsonld-image-unreachable` finding names the image URL as `og-image-bad` does, because the resource step that judges it knows the URL and the pages linking to it, not the node.

An image is skipped, never listed: `aria-hidden="true"` on it or an ancestor, an inline `display:none` or `visibility:hidden` on it or an ancestor, a `<noscript>` or `<template>` ancestor, a CSS background, or `alt=""` with 0 or 1 pixel dimensions. On the rendered sample the computed styles decide. An image with `alt=""`, `role="presentation"` or `role="none"` passes as decorative, and the report counts the decorative images as passes, because the HTML standard requires exactly `alt=""` for a decorative image and whether an image is decorative is a judgment only the author can make.

### Performance, weight 10

### AI access, weight 10

Google-Extended controls Gemini training and the grounding of Gemini Apps and Vertex AI in Google Search, and has no effect on Search itself; AI Overviews and AI Mode are served from the normal index and controlled by Googlebot's rules. The vendors separate training agents, search agents and user-fetch agents: blocking a training agent costs no visibility, blocking a search agent removes the site from that assistant's answers. Google-Extended is both, so it counts with the search agents: a block keeps the site out of the answers Gemini grounds in Google Search. OpenAI, Perplexity, Amazon, Meta and Google say their user-fetch agents may not follow robots.txt; Anthropic says Claude-User does. Applebot follows the Googlebot group when robots.txt has no Applebot group. Since 1 July 2026 Cloudflare manages AI traffic in three categories, Search, Agent and Training. Since 15 September 2026 its Block and "Block on pages with ads" settings also apply to crawlers that both search and train, Googlebot, Bingbot and Applebot included, while "Disallow AI Training" leaves them allowed for search; a new domain of a site with ads starts with Training on "Disallow AI Training" and Agent on "Block on pages with ads", and a new domain without ads allows all three. So a site can be unreadable to assistants through a setting the owner never chose.

### Accessibility

Accessibility is not a direct ranking factor. The audit scores the overlap with search: alt text, link names, the title, the viewport. It reports `<html lang>` and heading order as hints and leaves the rest of WCAG to accessibility tools.

## The report

The Audit section opens on the latest audit. The first card is the score with its band, the check count the score stands on, the date, the pages read and rendered, the time taken, and the difference from the previous audit: "+7 since 3 October, 12 findings fixed, 2 new", with "No change" in place of the number when the difference is 0. The previous audit is the last earlier one whose run finished, never a stopped, stalled or failed one, so an audit stopped in between does not hide the comparison. The comparison stands on the checks both audits ran: each audit's score is computed again from its own check results on those checks alone, with the score capped at 49 when that audit was gated, and the difference is the latest's minus the previous's; fixed and new count only the findings of those checks. So the difference can differ from the two scores in the history, when one audit ran a check the other did not. The difference and the two counts are computed when the audit is scored and stored on the audit, so the card reads three numbers; a finished audit with no previous audit, or whose previous audit was scored before the audits stored the checks they ran, shows no comparison. A partial audit says so in the card. When the CDN test did not run in full, the card says why in one line under the check count, by the outcome the audit stored, and never blames the site for a refusal of the browser, because a rate limit shared across stores, as Shopify's, refuses it too:

- `browser-refused`: "Citequill could not test AI search agents at your CDN. A browser from Citequill’s address did not get the home page, which a firewall or a rate limit can cause."
- `bot-refused`: "Citequill could not test AI search agents at your CDN. A browser got the home page and CitequillBot did not, so the test cannot tell a rule against AI agents from a rule against every bot."
- `spoof-refused`: "Citequill could not test AI search agents at your CDN. A claimed Googlebot or Bingbot did not get the home page, so your CDN checks crawlers by their address and may let the real agents through."
- no outcome: "Citequill could not finish the test of AI search agents at your CDN, so it does not count in the score."

When the site is a shell, the card's description says "Citequill read almost no text in the raw HTML of this site. Google renders JavaScript and some hosts send crawlers a prerendered copy, so the findings about the content may not hold for them." While an audit runs, the card shows its progress and a Stop button; a stalled or failed audit shows what went wrong and a button that runs it again, and a stopped audit shows its partial score with its band.

Three views of the findings, each a table the server filters, sorts and pages from the URL, as Pages is:

- by finding: the check, its severity and area, how many pages have a finding of it and how many of those are intended, the list of pages, what Google says, or in AI access the engine or study the rule comes from, with its link, how to fix it, and a button to mark it intended at the URL, selector or check grain
- by page: every finding of one URL, with the page's status, canonical, title and whether Citequill wrote it
- by image: every image with a finding, grouped by its subject, because a logo repeats on 300 pages and is fixed once: the image itself when the subject is a URL, the alts as found (absent, empty and whitespace told apart), the checks that failed on it, a CSS selector, a short HTML snippet, the ancestor that decided the verdict, the link or the figure, and how many pages hold it, the most repeated image first

A row opens the list of its findings on the same address, paged as the table is: the pages of a check, the findings of a page, or the pages an image is on, each with its marks. The tables show the latest audit whose run has ended, the partial findings of a stopped audit included; while an audit runs they show the audit before it, with its date, and offer no marks, because a mark recomputes only the latest audit.

Every finding carries the page URL, the subject (an image `src`, the `<use>` href or a short hash of an inline SVG or `role="img"` element, a link `href`, a sitemap URL), a CSS selector built from id, classes and tag so the same element has the same selector on every page, a short HTML snippet, and a detail, the JSON the check writes about what it saw; the audit keeps no page HTML. The report shows the detail as one line per key the customer can act on, each with a label from one table beside the texts of the checks: "Missing: strict-transport-security, content-security-policy" on `security-headers`, "Reasons: headings, words" on `mobile-content-differs`, "Code: CERT_HAS_EXPIRED" on `tls-invalid`, "Final URL: http://acme.com/" on `https-missing`, "Status: 404" on a broken link. A key with no label stays hidden: `reason`, which tells the halves of a check apart, `hops`, and the rest the audit keeps for itself. A check that caps its findings at 20 per page ends the line with "and 14 more" when the page had 34, and says nothing when every element is listed. A finding has a fingerprint, the hash of its check, page, subject and selector, and of its half on `assets-heavy`, whose two halves stand on one home page with neither a subject nor a selector, so two audits can be compared: fixed is in the previous and not in the latest, new is in the latest and not in the previous, and a finding marked intended in between is neither. A finding on a page Citequill wrote is labelled so, because it is a regression test of Citequill's own writer and template on a real site.

A filter by language narrows every view to one locale; a finding on the site itself, such as one on robots.txt or a sitemap, stays under every language, and the score stays the site's.

The history lists every audit with its score, band and counters. The owner opens any of them; the comparison is between the latest and the last finished one before it, because only they keep their findings. Older audits keep their score and counters.

## The site's pages

The list of the site's URLs has one home, `site_pages`, which the connect crawl starts and every audit updates: URL, locale, status, final URL, whether the page is indexable, whether robots.txt kept the audit from fetching it, its canonical, its title, its hreflang alternates from its tags and its `Link` header and the count of distinct alternate URLs by every method, an `href` that does not resolve counted by its text, its click depth and inbound link count, when it was first and last seen, and whether Citequill wrote it. `crawled_pages` keeps the content the facts come from.

A row describes the URL's own answer. A URL that redirects has the status of its first hop, the next URL as its redirect and the end of the chain as its final URL, and is not indexable. A page is indexable when it answers 200, robots.txt does not block it, and neither its `robots` or `googlebot` meta tag nor an `X-Robots-Tag` line for Googlebot or for every crawler says `noindex` or `none`. Its locale is the locale whose base URL holds it, else its `<html lang>`. Citequill wrote it when a published page's computed path is its URL, with or without the trailing slash. A writer checking internal links reads `site_pages` and the project's published pages, and a link may point only at a page whose latest observation, by the crawl or an audit, says indexable, canonical and 200. An audit that read the whole site marks the pages it did not meet as gone, so they stop being link targets; a partial or stopped audit leaves the pages it did not reach as they were.

## Measurements

Every audit records what it cost to run: pages read, pages rendered, duration, sandbox CPU seconds and GB-hours, requests made, 429 and 5xx answers from the host, and the cost in dollars, from the unit prices in one place beside the model prices. The cost goes to the run's `cost_usd`, so the column means what the run cost Citequill. The render module doubles as the measurement spike: the first start time of the sandbox image, which is how long `Sandbox.create()` takes, CPU seconds per rendered page, whether a detached process outlives the function that started it, and whether Cloudflare lets the bot through without the secret, all recorded in the research document and refreshed by every audit's own numbers. The sandbox's CPU seconds come from `activeCpuDurationMs`, which `stop()` returns, and its GB-hours are its vCPUs times 2 GB times its session time, rounded up to the minute, so neither is estimated.

## Delivery

The render module waits until a site in use is a shell, because every site in use today serves its pages in raw HTML, and rendering them finds nothing.
